Did the Virtual TA make learning easier and more effective?

Every descriptive and inferential result from the student survey, reported in full — including the null results, the internal contradictions in the data, and the findings that the study design cannot support.

n = 97 respondents 19 items analysed 13 hypothesis tests α = .05, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR Single course, single semester

Before reading the numbers

What the denominator is, and why it changes the answer

All 97 respondents answered every outcome question, including the 19 who reported never using the Virtual TA (18 “Never”, 1 “was not aware of it”). A percentage taken over all 97 therefore blends people describing an experience with people who did not have one. This report gives both denominators wherever the distinction changes the reading, and marks which one is in use.

Data-quality flags found in the source file

All 19 non-users still recorded a VTA purpose and a typical session length, and 17 of them selected “The Virtual TA didn’t affect my learning”. Either the questionnaire did not branch past the usage question, or “Never” was interpreted loosely. Their answers to experience-based items cannot be taken at face value.

Four items are labelled “select all that apply” but hold exactly one response each in all 97 rows — study strategies, perceived learning outcome, depth vs. superficial understanding, and self-reported adherence. They behave as forced single-choice items, so their percentages are competing shares of one pick, not independent endorsement rates. A student who agreed with three statements could register only one, which systematically depresses every option.

Two columns in the cleaned_data sheet — clarity and understanding — are entirely empty. They were declared but never populated, so no analysis used them.

Who answered

The sample skews toward one cohort: 52.6% Juniors, 23.7% Sophomores, 23.7% Seniors. Prior AI familiarity was concentrated in the middle — 52.6% “Moderate”, 27.8% “High”, 9.3% “Very high”, 9.3% “Low”. Before the course, 48.5% already used AI tools weekly or daily and only 4.1% had never used them. The response rate is not recorded in the data, so non-response bias cannot be estimated.

Theme 1

Student engagement

How much the Virtual TA was actually used: frequency, duration, timing, purpose, and how it fitted into study routines.

Reach was broad but shallow. Four in five students opened the Virtual TA at least once, yet the single most common pattern was using it once or twice in the entire semester (36.1%). Only 13 students (13.4%) used it weekly or more often.

When it was used

Use clustered around assessment rather than continuous study: 34.0% used it mostly near exams or deadlines, 27.8% only tried it once or twice, 17.5% mostly at the start of the course, 16.5% mostly mid-semester, and 4.1% used it consistently throughout the semester.

Concept clarification led, but “just curious / playing with it” was selected by 36 of 97 students — the third most-cited purpose overall. Exploratory use is a substantial share of the recorded engagement, which is consistent with the large group who used the tool only once or twice.

How it fitted into study routines

Forced single choice, n = 97. Most students placed the tool after their own effort: 59.8% used it mainly after trying problems themselves; 13.4% used it mainly before attempting them. On the risk side, 12.4% said they sometimes accepted its answers without critically evaluating them and 6.2% said it made them more likely to procrastinate; 8.2% said it encouraged them to attempt more challenging problems.

The VTA-generated FAQ

The FAQ built from student questions reached less than the chatbot itself: 40.2% were unaware of it or did not use it. Among those who engaged, 30.9% of all respondents said it helped them understand the topic better, 25.8% that it clarified common confusions, 22.7% that it was a useful quick review, 18.6% that it raised perspectives they had not considered, and 16.5% that it helped them prepare for assessments. 14.4% reviewed it only briefly, and 4.1% found it too basic.

What this shows

Engagement was not explained by seniority or by prior experience with AI tools. Sophomores, Juniors and Seniors used the Virtual TA at statistically indistinguishable rates (median intensity 1.0, 1.0 and 2.0 respectively), and students who arrived already fluent with AI tools did not use it more. T4 is a consistency check on the instrument, not a finding: two questions about pre-course AI habits agree with each other, as they should.

What this does not show

T1 is close to definitional — frequency and session length are two measurements of the same underlying exposure, so a strong correlation is expected and carries little information. And seven null results at n = 97 are not evidence of no effect: with three level groups of 23, 51 and 23, only a large difference would have been detectable. These are inconclusive, not negative.

Theme 2

Student learning

Reported effects on understanding, reasoning and depth — separated from effects on speed and workload, which appear under the next theme.

This is the item where the denominator matters most. Across all 97 respondents, “didn’t affect my learning” (37.1%) nearly matches “understood concepts more deeply” (47.4%). Restricted to the 78 who used the tool, the null response falls to 24.4% while the deeper-understanding response rises to 56.4% — because 17 of the 19 non-users selected the null option. Neither figure is more correct than the other; they answer different questions.

Forced to pick one statement, the cohort split almost exactly in half: 48.5% chose a statement describing improved reasoning (47 students) and 51.5% chose one describing a risk to their own understanding (50 students). The single most-selected statement of the four is the concern about long-term dependence.

Among the 78 users the balance shifts positive — 30.8% “changed how I think”, 28.2% “ask why”, 24.4% “worry about dependence”, 16.7% “felt I understood more than I did”. Among the 19 non-users it is almost entirely negative (52.6% worry, 42.1% false confidence), though what a non-user’s answer to this item means is unclear.

24.7% reported encountering flawed responses and 19.6% did not, but 55.7% sat on “Neutral” — a majority declining to take a position. This is the survey’s only Likert item, so it supports no scale reliability estimate (Cronbach’s α needs multiple items), and a single item with this much central clustering is weak evidence in either direction.

Self-reported adherence to course rules

Forced single choice, n = 97. 53.6% said they always used the tool in ways consistent with course rules and 32.0% that they used AI mainly to check work they had already done. 8.2% acknowledged using it in ways they suspected the instructor might not approve of, and 6.2% said they submitted AI-generated solutions with minimal modification (9.0% and 7.7% respectively among users). These are self-reports on a sensitive question and should be read as a lower bound.

What this shows

T9 is a clean null: students who reported understanding concepts more deeply gave identical ratings on the accuracy concern (both medians 3.0; means 3.04 vs. 3.10). Perceiving a learning benefit and noticing flawed answers are unrelated in this sample. T12 is the more consequential null — among users, heavier use had no relationship whatever with reporting deeper understanding (ρ = −0.001). Confidence did rise modestly with use (T13).

What this does not show

Every learning outcome here is self-perceived. No grade, test score, or other objective measure appears anywhere in the dataset, so nothing in this section can speak to whether learning actually improved — only to what students believed. T12 and T13 are correlational and cross-sectional; a student who felt more confident may simply have kept using the tool.

Theme 3

Learning benefit

Efficiency, workload, perceived outcomes, and whether students would want the tool again — the practical payoff as students assessed it.

Efficiency is where the tool registered most strongly: 60 of 78 users (76.9%) selected at least one efficiency benefit. The most-cited effect — reducing time stuck — is also the narrowest claim in the list. Only 23.1% of users reported substituting the Virtual TA for office hours, peers or other resources, so displacement of existing support was limited in this cohort.

The most-chosen outcome statement explicitly decouples learning from grades. Only 11.3% attributed a grade effect to the Virtual TA — the lowest share of the four options, and the only one making a causal claim about performance.

Across all 97 respondents, 58.8% would recommend the Virtual TA and 11.3% would not; 54.6% would like one in other technical courses and 14.4% would not. The non-user confidence intervals are wide enough (n = 19) to overlap the user estimates, so the apparent gap between the two groups is suggestive rather than established.

The largest effects in this study are partly structural

T14–T16 compare students who used the Virtual TA against students who did not, on whether they reported a benefit from it. A student who never used the tool has no basis on which to report a benefit, and the comparison categories are defined by the absence of a selection — “No benefit” simply means the benefit option was not ticked. The contingency table makes this visible: 18 of 19 non-users selected “none of the above” for efficiency, against 18 of 78 users.

These tests therefore largely confirm that non-users do not report benefits. The odds ratio of 11.0 should not be read as an effect of the Virtual TA on learning. The dose–response results within users only (T12, T13, T17) avoid this problem and are the more informative comparisons, because everyone in them used the tool.

What this shows

Restricted to users, the pattern separates cleanly by outcome type. Heavier use tracks efficiency gains (ρ = 0.318, p = .005; median intensity 2.0 among those reporting a benefit vs. 1.0 among those not) and modestly tracks confidence (ρ = 0.261), but has no relationship at all with deeper understanding (ρ = −0.001). Students who used it more also wanted it more (T18, T19) without reporting more quality problems (T20).

What this does not show

Direction is unrecoverable: students who found the tool useful plausibly used it more, rather than use producing the benefit. T18 and T19 may be measuring enthusiasm, not outcomes. Six of the seven exploratory tests were chosen after seeing the data, and several contingency tables carry expected cell counts below 5, so their χ² approximations are unreliable.

Constraints on every number above

What this survey cannot answer

  • No objective outcome measure exists in the data. Grades, scores and completion times are absent. Every result is a self-report of perception, so the survey can establish what students believed about their learning and nothing about their learning itself.
  • The design is cross-sectional with no comparison group. There is no pre/post measurement and no cohort without a Virtual TA. Causal language is unsupported for every finding, including the significant ones.
  • Non-users answered experience-based items. All 19 recorded a session length and a usage purpose despite reporting no use, so any percentage over all 97 on an experience item contains responses that cannot be interpreted.
  • Four items forced a single choice among statements that are not mutually exclusive. The depth, outcome, strategy and adherence percentages are shares of a forced pick and understate how many students would have endorsed each statement.
  • Only one Likert item was asked, and 55.7% answered it “Neutral”. No multi-item scale exists, so no reliability coefficient (α or ω), factor analysis or scale-level comparison is possible — the methods in Chapters 8 and 9 of the project handbook could not be applied.
  • No regression model was fitted. All inference is bivariate, so none of the associations is adjusted for the others; the confounding between use intensity, enthusiasm and prior AI habit remains unmodelled.
  • Sensitive items invite under-reporting. The 6.2% who reported submitting AI-generated solutions with minimal modification and the 8.2% who reported use they thought the instructor would not approve are lower bounds under social-desirability pressure.
  • The response rate is unknown and the sample comes from one course in one semester, skewed toward Juniors (52.6%). Generalisation beyond this cohort is not supported.
  • Multiplicity is only partly controlled. BH correction was applied across the six pre-specified χ² tests; the Spearman set and the seven exploratory cross-checks were adjusted separately, and exploratory findings need replication before being treated as results.

The four sentences the data does support

Roughly four in five students tried the Virtual TA, but engagement was shallow and assessment-driven — a third used it once or twice, sessions were mostly under 15 minutes, and 4.1% used it consistently. Among users, a majority reported help with understanding concepts (56.4%) and with getting unstuck (56.4%), while about a quarter reported no effect on either. When forced to choose one statement about depth, the cohort split evenly between improved reasoning and worry about dependence. And within users, more use went with more efficiency and more confidence but showed no relationship with deeper understanding.
